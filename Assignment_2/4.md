# 4. What is Handoff? Explain different types of Handoff strategies in cellular systems.

**Handoff** (also referred to as **handover**) is the essential process in cellular telecommunications of transferring an ongoing voice call or data session from one base station's coverage area to another without interrupting the user's connection. 

As a mobile subscriber travels across a city, the radio signal strength between their device and the serving base station naturally weakens. Simultaneously, the signal strength from an adjacent base station begins to rise. To prevent the connection from dropping, the **Mobile Switching Center (MSC)** coordinates a seamless transfer of the active call to a new traffic channel belonging to the neighboring base station.

To manage this transition under different network architectures, operators utilize four primary handoff strategies:

### **1. Hard Handoff**
Known mathematically as the **"break-before-make"** approach, a hard handoff completely terminates the connection with the current base station a fraction of a second *before* establishing a connection with the new target base station.
*   **How it works:** The radio link to the serving cell is severed, and the device instantly tunes to the frequency of the adjacent cell. Because this transition happens in milliseconds, the brief break in connectivity is generally imperceptible to the user.
*   **Compatibility:** This strategy is predominantly used in **FDMA** and **TDMA** (such as older 2G/GSM) networks where a mobile device cannot transmit and receive on multiple frequencies simultaneously.
*   **Pros & Cons:** It is highly efficient and **cheaper to implement** because a device only needs one active channel at any given moment. However, the connection quality is slightly lower, and users may occasionally experience a minor audible delay during the switch.

### **2. Soft Handoff**
Often called the **"make-before-break"** approach, a soft handoff ensures the mobile device connects to the new base station *before* dropping its connection to the old one.
*   **How it works:** The mobile station maintains simultaneous active radio links with two or more base stations at the same time. If one of the links suffers a sudden power loss, the standby link is already active, ensuring a seamless transition.
*   **Compatibility:** This requires advanced processing and is used extensively in **CDMA** and **W-CDMA** (3G) networks. It is physically **incompatible with standard GSM or LTE** networks.
*   **Pros & Cons:** It offers the **highest possible connection quality** and eliminates switching delays entirely. However, it is significantly more expensive because the network must allocate multiple channel resources to a single user during the transition.

### **3. Delayed Handoff**
A delayed handoff occurs as a fallback mechanism when a user moves toward a new cell, but **no neighboring base station is currently available** to accept the call transfer.
*   **How it works:** Instead of performing an immediate switch, the network forces the call to remain on its current weakening channel. The call continues to operate in a degraded state until the signal strength hits a critical threshold, at which point the call is dropped.
*   **Typical Scenario:** This generally happens when a user travels out of a network's overall coverage boundary or crosses a "dead spot" where the signal reach is extremely low.

### **4. Mobile-Assisted Handoff (MAHO)**
Mobile-Assisted Handoff distributes the technical burden of the handoff decision between the network and the user equipment.
*   **How it works:** The mobile device continuously measures the signal strength of all neighboring base stations and reports these real-time measurements back to its current serving base station. The network uses this user-provided data to decide exactly when and where to route the handoff for the cleanest possible signal.
*   **Compatibility:** MAHO is widely deployed in TDMA-based digital systems, such as GSM.
